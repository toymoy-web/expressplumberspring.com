Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Do You Need?

By the Spring Express Plumbing Team · Published 2026-09-22 · Updated 2026-09-22

Snaking uses a flexible cable to physically break through and pull out a specific clog. Hydro jetting uses high-pressure water to clean the entire inside diameter of a pipe, removing not just the clog but the buildup along the walls that led to it. For a single, isolated clog, snaking is usually the faster, less expensive fix. For recurring clogs or significant buildup, hydro jetting addresses the underlying cause rather than just the immediate blockage.

How Each Method Actually Works

A drain snake, also called an auger, is a flexible metal cable fed into the pipe until it reaches the clog. Depending on the tool and the clog, it either breaks the blockage apart, hooks it and pulls it back out, or both. It's targeted and mechanical: it deals with what's directly in front of it.

A hydro jetter sends water through a specialized nozzle at very high pressure, scouring the entire interior surface of the pipe as it moves through, not just the spot where a clog happens to be sitting. That's the core difference: snaking removes an obstruction, hydro jetting cleans the pipe itself.

Comparing the Two Directly

Neither method is universally better, they solve different problems.

When Snaking Is the Right Call

A single slow or clogged drain with no history of repeat problems is usually a good candidate for snaking. It's faster, less expensive, and gets water moving again without needing to address the whole line. Most single-fixture clogs, a clogged kitchen sink or a slow bathtub drain, fall into this category.

When Hydro Jetting Makes More Sense

A drain that clogs repeatedly despite being snaked each time usually has ongoing buildup along the pipe walls that snaking never actually addresses, since a snake clears a path through the clog rather than cleaning the surrounding surface. Grease buildup in a kitchen line, tree root intrusion in a sewer line, and pipes that haven't been cleaned in years are all better matched to hydro jetting than repeated snaking.

If a hydro jetting assessment reveals the pipe itself has significant damage rather than just buildup, that shifts the conversation toward sewer line repair or, for a less severe blockage that's still recurring, ongoing drain cleaning service instead.

What an Assessment Looks Like Before Either Method

A camera inspection before committing to either method is genuinely useful, especially for a recurring problem rather than a first-time clog. It shows whether the issue is truly buildup that jetting would clear, a specific obstruction snaking would remove, or structural damage that neither method addresses on its own.

This step matters most for older pipes or a history of repeated clogs in the same location, since it prevents spending on the wrong solution for what's actually happening inside the line.

Combining Both Methods

These two approaches aren't always an either-or choice. On some jobs, a plumber snakes a line first to clear an immediate blockage and restore flow, then follows up with hydro jetting to clean the full pipe once water is moving again. This sequence gets a blocked drain functional again quickly while still addressing the underlying buildup that likely caused the clog in the first place.

Whether this combined approach makes sense for your situation depends on the severity of the blockage and what the initial assessment reveals. That's exactly the kind of judgment call worth discussing directly with whoever's doing the work rather than assuming one method or the other in advance.

Which Areas of the Home See Each Method Most

Kitchen sink drains, given how often grease and food residue accumulate there over time, are a common candidate for periodic hydro jetting even without an active clog, purely as preventive maintenance in households that cook frequently. Bathroom drains, more often dealing with hair and soap buildup than grease, tend to respond well to snaking for isolated clogs.

Main sewer lines sit in their own category entirely, given their length, depth, and the higher stakes of a full blockage. A camera inspection paired with the appropriate method, jetting for buildup, root-cutting attachments for intrusion, or repair for structural damage, is the standard, thorough approach for this specific part of the system.

Common Questions

Is hydro jetting safe for all pipes?
It requires the pipe to be in reasonably sound structural condition first. A severely corroded or already-cracked pipe can be damaged further by the water pressure, which is why an inspection typically happens before jetting, not after.
How often should hydro jetting be done as maintenance?
For homes with a history of grease buildup or tree root issues, some plumbers recommend it every year or two as preventive maintenance. For a home with no recurring problems, it's more of an as-needed service than a routine one.
Can I rent a drain snake and do this myself?
For a simple, accessible clog, some homeowners do. For anything recurring, for a main sewer line, or if you're unsure what's actually causing the clog, a professional assessment avoids the risk of pushing a clog further in or missing a bigger underlying issue.
Does hydro jetting work on a completely blocked pipe?
Often yes, since the pressure can break through many blockages the same way a snake would, then continue cleaning the surrounding pipe. For a fully collapsed section of pipe rather than a blockage, jetting isn't the right tool and repair or replacement is needed instead.
Is hydro jetting more expensive because it takes longer than snaking?
Not necessarily longer, but it typically involves more specialized equipment and a more thorough process, which factors into the higher cost. For a straightforward single clog, snaking is usually both faster and cheaper.
Can hydro jetting prevent future clogs, not just clear existing ones?
Yes, that's actually one of its main advantages over snaking. By clearing buildup along the entire pipe wall rather than just the immediate obstruction, it genuinely extends the time before the next clog forms compared to repeated spot-snaking of the same recurring problem.
Will hydro jetting damage older pipes that are already somewhat fragile?
It can, which is exactly why an inspection typically comes first. A plumber adjusts pressure based on what the pipe's condition can actually handle, and for genuinely fragile pipe, a gentler approach or a different method entirely may be recommended instead.
How soon after hydro jetting can I use the drains normally again?
Immediately in most cases. There's no curing time or waiting period involved at all, once the service is complete, the line is clear and fully functional right away.
