Is a Running Toilet an Emergency? A Spring, TX Answer

By the Spring Express Plumbing Team · Published 2026-09-22 · Updated 2026-09-22

A running toilet is not a plumbing emergency in the sense of an active flood or an urgent safety risk, but it deserves prompt attention because of what it costs. A worn flapper can waste 200 gallons a day, and left alone for a full billing cycle, that adds up to a real, avoidable increase on your water bill.

Most causes are a faulty flapper, a misadjusted float, or a fill valve that isn't shutting off completely, and many are fixable without a plumber.

Why a Toilet Runs Continuously

Every toilet tank uses the same basic cycle: press the handle, the flapper lifts, water rushes into the bowl, the flapper reseals, and the fill valve refills the tank until the float signals it's full. A running toilet means somewhere in that cycle isn't sealing or shutting off correctly.

The flapper, a rubber seal at the bottom of the tank, is the most common culprit. Rubber degrades over years of constant water contact, and once it warps or cracks, it stops sealing evenly, letting water trickle into the bowl continuously.

How to Tell What's Actually Wrong

Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper isn't sealing. If the water level in the tank sits right at or above the overflow tube, the float is set too high or the fill valve isn't shutting off.

SymptomLikely Cause
Color test shows leak into bowlWorn or misaligned flapper
Water level at or above overflow tubeFloat set too high, or fill valve not shutting off
Handle feels loose or doesn't fully trigger flushChain too long or disconnected
Runs intermittently, not constantlySlow flapper leak or a fill valve seating issue

What You Can Try Yourself

A flapper is inexpensive and straightforward to replace: shut off the toilet's own supply valve, drain the tank, and swap the old flapper for a new one matched to your toilet's make. Adjusting the float, either the ball or clip depending on your fill valve type, to sit slightly lower often fixes an overfilling tank without any parts needed.

If neither fixes it, or the fill valve itself is worn out and won't shut off cleanly, that's when it's worth calling in toilet repair and installation rather than continuing to replace parts one at a time.

When It's Worth Calling a Plumber

If you've replaced the flapper and adjusted the float and the toilet still runs, there may be a cracked overflow tube, a valve that's failing internally, or an issue with the toilet itself rather than its parts. A toilet that runs intermittently in a way that's hard to diagnose, or one connected to a suspiciously high water bill that hasn't resolved with simple fixes, is also worth a professional look rather than continued guessing.

Older Toilets and Original Parts

Homes in this area built more than a decade or two ago sometimes still have original toilet hardware, parts that have simply never needed replacing until now. Original fill valves and flappers from that era don't always match current standard sizes exactly, which can make a hardware-store replacement not fit quite right even when it looks correct in the box.

This is a genuinely common source of frustration: a homeowner replaces a flapper, the toilet keeps running, and the actual issue turns out to be a mismatched fit rather than a defective part. Bringing the old part along when shopping for a replacement, or having a plumber match it directly, avoids this trial and error entirely.

A Running Toilet in a Multi-Bathroom Home

If you have more than one bathroom, it's worth checking each toilet individually rather than assuming a running sound is coming from the one you can hear it from. Sound travels through shared walls and plumbing chases in ways that can make a running toilet in one bathroom seem like it's coming from a different one entirely, especially at night when the house is otherwise quiet.

This matters because fixing the wrong toilet, or assuming a sound has stopped when it's actually just paused, wastes both time and water. A quick check of every toilet's tank, not just the one you suspect, is worth the extra few minutes before concluding a repair actually worked.

It's also worth checking guest bathrooms and any toilet that isn't used daily. A running toilet in a rarely-used bathroom can go unnoticed for weeks or months simply because nobody's in the room to hear it. That's one of the more common sources of an unexplained high water bill in homes with more than one or two bathrooms.

What to Do If the Toilet Is Overflowing

Do this first

  • Close the supply valve behind the toilet by turning it clockwise.
  • Lift the tank lid and hold the flapper closed if water keeps rising.
  • Put towels down and keep the bathroom door closed.
  • Call a plumber if other drains are slow or backing up too.

Test It With Food Coloring

EPA's WaterSense program says to put a drop of food coloring in the toilet tank and wait. If any color shows up in the bowl after 10 minutes, you have a leak.

EPA also says fixing easily corrected household leaks can save about 10 percent on water bills. If your bill climbs in Spring and the toilet tests clean, move on to the meter test.

Common Questions

How much can a running toilet actually cost me?
A worn flapper wasting 200 gallons a day adds up to roughly 6,000 gallons a month. Depending on your water and sewer rate, that can mean a genuinely noticeable jump on a single billing cycle, sometimes tens of dollars or more added on top of your normal usage, not just a minor increase easy to overlook.
Can I keep using the toilet while it's running?
Yes, a running toilet is still fully usable. It's a waste and cost issue, not a functional or safety one, which is why it doesn't count as a true emergency even though it's worth fixing promptly rather than letting it run indefinitely.
Is it normal for a toilet to run for a few seconds after flushing?
Yes, a brief refill period after flushing is completely normal. The concern is water running continuously between flushes, or restarting on its own a few minutes after the tank should have settled.
How long do toilet flappers usually last?
A few years on average, though water quality and flapper material both affect that. If your toilet is older and has never had the flapper replaced, that's often the first thing worth checking regardless of other symptoms present at the time.
Will a running toilet eventually stop on its own?
No, a worn part doesn't repair itself. It may get gradually worse or stay at a steady rate, but it won't resolve without either a parts replacement or, in some cases, a full toilet replacement if the tank or bowl itself has developed a crack.
Is a running toilet ever a sign of a bigger plumbing issue?
Occasionally. A toilet that starts running right after other plumbing work nearby, or alongside other fixtures acting unusually, can point to a pressure change or a shared supply line issue rather than a simple worn part in that one toilet. Mentioning any recent work to a plumber is worth doing.
Does the type of water heater we have affect toilet running issues?
No, these are unrelated systems. A running toilet is entirely about the tank's internal fill and flush mechanism, not anything connected to how your home heats water, so troubleshooting one never points toward the other.
How do I test a toilet for a silent leak?
Add a drop of food coloring to the tank, wait 10 minutes without flushing, and look in the bowl. If color appears, the flapper is leaking and the toilet needs a repair.
