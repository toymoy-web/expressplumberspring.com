Repiping & Whole-Home Pipe Replacement in Spring, TX

Spring Express Plumbing repipes older homes across Spring, Texas, most often replacing galvanized steel or polybutylene supply lines that were standard in homes built before the mid-1990s. Both materials fail from the inside in ways you usually cannot see until a leak shows up.

Most repiping calls do not start as a request to repipe the whole house. They start as a series of small leaks or a sudden drop in water pressure, and repiping comes up once it is clear the underlying pipe material is the actual problem, not any one fixture.

2

Frequent Small Leaks or Falling Water Pressure?

Galvanized pipe rusts from the inside, narrowing the interior diameter over decades until pressure drops noticeably, especially at upstairs fixtures. Polybutylene, common in homes built roughly 1978 to 1995, becomes brittle with age and chlorine exposure and tends to fail at fittings rather than mid-pipe. If you are patching a new pinhole leak every few months, or pressure has dropped gradually over a couple of years rather than all at once, the pipe material itself is usually the real cause, and further individual repairs mostly delay the same conversation.

3

How We Assess a Repipe

We check the home's construction era, the visible pipe material at the water heater and under sinks, and any history of prior leak repairs before recommending a full repipe over continued spot fixes.

Most repipes in single-story Spring homes are done through walls and attic space with minimal drywall cutting, using PEX tubing run from a central manifold. Copper is available where local code or personal preference calls for it, at a higher material cost.

A whole-home repipe in unincorporated Harris or Montgomery County typically requires a permit and a final inspection before the walls go back together, which we handle as part of the job rather than leaving it to you to sort out afterward. Most PEX manufacturers back their tubing with long warranties, often two to three decades, which is worth confirming against whatever material your installer actually uses.

4

PEX, Copper, or a Partial Repipe

Full PEX repipe

The most common choice today. Flexible tubing routes around obstacles with fewer joints than rigid pipe, which means fewer future failure points.

Copper repipe

A higher material cost with a very long service life, and the right choice if your homeowners association or personal preference calls for it.

Partial repipe

Replacing just the sections showing active failure, worth considering on a newer home with only one problem area rather than home-wide degraded pipe.

✓

What to Do Before Repipe Day

Clear access to attics, crawlspaces, and under-sink cabinets the day before so the crew isn't waiting on you to move boxes.
Plan for water to be off in short windows throughout the day rather than one long outage. We'll tell you the actual sequence that morning.
Move anything valuable or fragile away from walls where a small amount of drywall or texture work may be needed.
Keep pets in a separate room or offsite for the day. Open wall cavities and ladders are not pet-friendly.
Have your homeowners insurance policy number handy if this repipe is following a documented leak claim.

Common Questions

How long does a whole-home repipe take?
Most single-story Spring homes take two to four days depending on size and access. We sequence the work so water is only off for short windows, not the entire project.
Is polybutylene pipe actually dangerous?
It is not a safety hazard in the sense of gas or fire, but it does fail at a meaningfully higher rate than copper or PEX as it ages, especially at fittings, which is why insurers increasingly ask about it on older homes.
Do I need to repipe the whole house or just the section that's leaking?
That depends on whether the leaks are isolated or the pipe material itself is failing broadly. We will look at the actual pattern of failures with you before recommending a full repipe over targeted repairs.
